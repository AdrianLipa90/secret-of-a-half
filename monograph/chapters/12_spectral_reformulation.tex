\chapter{Spectral Reformulation and the Real-Zero Mechanism}
\label{chap:spectral}

\section{Hilbert--P\'olya logic}

In the centred coordinate $s=1/2+iz$, RH is equivalent to all zeros of $\XiR(z)$ being real. The strongest familiar mechanism for real spectral values is self-adjointness.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional spectral theorem]
\label{thm:spectral-conditional}
Suppose there exists a self-adjoint operator $H$ on a Hilbert space and an entire spectral determinant $D(z)$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \XiR(z)=e^{a+bz}D(z),
 \label{eq:spectral-factorization}
\end{equation}
where the exponential factor is non-zero and the zeros of $D$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $H$, with multiplicity. Then RH holds.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator is real. The non-vanishing exponential does not alter zeros, so every zero of $\XiR$ is real. By \cref{eq:rh-real-xi-zeros}, every non-trivial zeta zero lies on $\Re s=1/2$.
\end{proof}

The theorem captures the Hilbert--P\'olya idea. Many quantum-chaotic analogies and operator proposals are motivated by zero statistics \citep{montgomery1973,odlyzko1987,berrykeating1999,keatingsnaith2000}. No accepted operator satisfying \cref{eq:spectral-factorization} is known.

\section{How the half enters spectrally}

Self-adjointness does not derive the number $1/2$ from scratch. The functional equation first determines the centred coordinate. Specifically,
\[
 z=-i(s-1/2)
\]
turns the anti-linear involution $\J$ into complex conjugation. The half is therefore the coordinate shift that converts zeta symmetry into spectral reality. The operator then supplies the missing orbit-collapse principle.

This separation mirrors the information-spinor theorem:
\begin{align*}
 \text{functional symmetry}&\longrightarrow\text{centre }1/2,\\
 \text{self-adjointness or positive cancellation}&\longrightarrow\text{reality/fixation}.
\end{align*}
The two frameworks differ in the mechanism of the second arrow.

\section{Two-level compression of a spectral problem}

Suppose a candidate operator $H$ carries an involution or grading that decomposes the Hilbert space into two complementary sectors. Near an eigenvalue, an effective two-dimensional subspace may be spanned by sector components. In such a compression, the determinant condition can reduce to cancellation of two amplitudes. The information-spinor model may then be interpreted as an effective local description of a richer spectral object.

This perspective avoids requiring the entire zeta function to be literally a qubit amplitude. Instead:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the full operator provides arithmetic and spectral content;
  \item symmetry supplies a two-sector decomposition;
  \item a zero mode is compressed to a balanced two-component state;
  \item the half-axis expresses equality of sector norms under self-duality.
\end{enumerate}
This is a plausible architecture but still requires construction.

\section{A $2\times2$ toy Hamiltonian}

Consider the Hermitian matrix
\begin{equation}
 H(x,t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
  x & q(t)\\
  \overline{q(t)} & -x
 \end{pmatrix},
 \label{eq:toy-H}
\end{equation}
where $x=\sigma-1/2$. Its eigenvalues are
\[
 E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{x^2+|q(t)|^2}.
\]
A zero eigenvalue requires $x=0$ and $q(t)=0$. Thus a Hermitian two-level model separates the horizontal balance condition from the vertical quantization condition exactly as desired.

The determinant is
\begin{equation}
 \det H(x,t)=-x^2-|q(t)|^2\le0,
 \label{eq:toy-det}
\end{equation}
and vanishes only on the critical line at quantized values of $t$. If one could derive a function $q(t)$ whose zeros are the zeta ordinates and embed this matrix in a canonical operator family, RH would be automatic.

As written, the toy model is not a construction because choosing $q(t)=\XiR(t)$ imports the critical-line restriction and does not address off-axis zeros. Its value is to display the form of a coercive Hermitian mechanism: off-axis displacement contributes the positive term $x^2$ and cannot be cancelled by a real spectrum.

\section{Non-Hermitian deformation and off-axis zeros}

If self-adjointness is broken, let
\[
 H_\epsilon(x,t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
  x+i\epsilon & q(t)\\
  \overline{q(t)} & -x+i\epsilon
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Complex eigenvalues become possible. This suggests an interpretation of off-axis displacement as non-Hermitian leakage or failure of positivity. Such language is heuristic unless a genuine zeta operator is present, but it provides a falsifiable signature for candidate numerical models: any mechanism claiming RH should show exactly where Hermiticity enters and how it excludes horizontal displacement.

\section{Spectral determinant requirements}

Infinite-dimensional determinants require care. A valid proposal must specify:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the operator domain and proof of self-adjointness;
  \item discreteness or regularization of the relevant spectrum;
  \item the determinant definition and convergence class;
  \item the non-vanishing prefactor relating it to $\XiR$;
  \item multiplicities and possible continuous-spectrum contributions;
  \item arithmetic information explaining the prime side of the explicit formula.
\end{itemize}
Many attractive formal Hamiltonians fail at one of these points.

\section{Random-matrix evidence and its limit}

The local statistics of high zeta zeros resemble eigenvalue statistics of random Hermitian matrices, a connection initiated by Montgomery's pair correlation and developed through extensive numerical and theoretical work \citep{montgomery1973,odlyzko1987,keatingsnaith2000}. This is strong evidence for a spectral structure. Statistical agreement does not identify the operator and does not prove every zero is on the line.

For the present ansatz, random-matrix behaviour supports the idea that $t$ is a spectral coordinate, while the binary balance coordinate controls the horizontal axis. It does not directly support the specific entropy or spinor mapping.

\section{Integration with the information-spinor theorem}

A spectral realization could imply the information-spinor conditions rather than the reverse. Suppose $H$ has a swap symmetry and a zero mode decomposes into two sectors. If the symmetry makes the sector norms equal and the eigenvalue equation imposes opposite detector phase, the zero mode becomes the state $\ket{-}$ in the effective sector basis. The cancellation theorem then appears as the two-level shadow of self-adjoint spectral reality.

This integration is conceptually attractive because it assigns distinct tasks:
\begin{description}
  \item[Zeta functional equation:] centres the coordinate at $1/2$.
  \item[Self-adjoint operator:] makes the spectral coordinate real.
  \item[Sector symmetry:] produces binary equal norms.
  \item[Zero-mode equation:] produces destructive phase.
  \item[Entropy:] measures deviation from sector balance.
\end{description}
The architecture is coherent; the operator remains unknown.
